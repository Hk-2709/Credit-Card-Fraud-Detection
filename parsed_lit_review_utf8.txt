LITERATURE REVIEWCredit Card Fraud Detection Using Machine LearningA Comprehensive Review of Research Papers (2019–2025)1. IntroductionCredit card fraud represents one of the most economically damaging and technically challenging problems in modern financial systems. With global card fraud losses exceeding USD 32 billion annually and rising sharply due to the proliferation of e-commerce and contactless payments, the need for automated, intelligent fraud detection systems has never been more urgent. Traditional rule-based systems have proven inadequate in the face of evolving fraud tactics, generating excessive false positives while failing to catch novel fraudulent patterns. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have emerged as the dominant paradigms for addressing this challenge, offering the ability to learn complex non-linear patterns from large-scale transactional data.This literature review systematically surveys 44 research papers published between 2019 and 2025, focusing on the application of ML, DL, graph neural networks, federated learning, and explainable AI techniques to credit card fraud detection. Each paper is analysed along the dimensions of research aim, objectives, datasets employed, techniques applied, and key shortcomings. The review is contextualised within the scope of a capstone project using the widely-benchmarked ULB Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset, targeting a PR-AUC of greater than 0.95 through robust imbalance handling, feature engineering, and comparative model evaluation.2. Scope and Methodology of the Review2.1 Search StrategyResearch papers were sourced from IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Springer Link, Elsevier ScienceDirect, arXiv, MDPI, PeerJ, and ResearchGate. Search terms included combinations of: “credit card fraud detection”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “class imbalance”, “SMOTE”, “GAN”, “graph neural network”, “federated learning”, and “explainable AI”. Only papers published in peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings, or established preprint repositories from 2019 onward were included.2.2 Inclusion and Exclusion CriteriaIncluded: Papers from 2019–2025 with empirical evaluation on credit card fraud datasetsIncluded: Survey and systematic review papers covering the period with clear methodologyIncluded: Papers proposing novel architectures, resampling strategies, or evaluation frameworksExcluded: Papers without experimental validation or published before 2019Excluded: Purely theoretical papers not applied to fraud detection2.3 Classification FrameworkEach paper was categorised by: (1) primary objective, (2) ML/DL methodology, (3) dataset used and its accessibility (public/private), and (4) identified shortcomings relating to scalability, interpretability, class imbalance, concept drift, or reproducibility.3. Analysis and Discussion6.1 Dominance of the ULB DatasetThe ULB European Credit Card dataset accounts for the vast majority of reviewed studies, which, while enabling consistent benchmarking, introduces publication bias. Results on this 2013 dataset may not reflect modern fraud patterns, emphasising the need for more diverse and recent public datasets. The IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection dataset (Kaggle, 2019) provides richer features and represents a more realistic benchmark for graph-based approaches.6.2 Class Imbalance HandlingClass imbalance (fraud: ~0.17% in ULB) is the single most-cited challenge across all reviewed papers. SMOTE remains the dominant technique (used in 60%+ of papers), though its tendency to generate noise in sparse regions is well-documented. ADASYN and Borderline-SMOTE offer partial improvements. GAN-based oversampling represents the current frontier but introduces training instability and computational overhead. Cost-sensitive learning (class_weight='balanced', cost matrices) provides a model-level alternative that avoids synthetic sample generation but requires careful cost specification.6.3 Evaluation Metric InconsistenciesA critical finding is the inconsistent use of evaluation metrics. Accuracy is misleading on imbalanced datasets and yet continues to appear in 30%+ of reviewed papers as the primary metric. PR-AUC (Precision-Recall Area Under Curve) is the most informative metric for fraud detection, as it accounts for both precision and recall under imbalance. F1-score at optimal threshold and ROC-AUC are widely reported. Notably, papers that preserve the original class imbalance during evaluation (as reviewed in the MDPI Computers 2025 systematic review) report significantly lower performance numbers than those that evaluate on resampled test sets, reflecting a key reproducibility concern.6.4 Emergence of Graph and Federated ApproachesA significant trend in 2022–2025 is the adoption of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to model relational fraud patterns between users, merchants, and transactions. Heterogeneous GNN, CaT-GNN, and graph-attention-based federated models represent cutting-edge approaches that outperform tabular ML on datasets with rich relational features. However, these approaches are computationally intensive, require specialised graph construction pipelines, and rely heavily on private or restricted datasets, limiting reproducibility.6.5 Explainability GapDespite growing regulatory pressure (e.g., EU AI Act, GDPR Article 22), only a minority of reviewed papers incorporate explainability components such as SHAP or LIME. The opacity of ensemble and deep learning models remains a significant barrier to real-world deployment in regulated financial environments. Papers integrating XAI (e.g., Yadav et al. 2023) show that SHAP-based feature importance can identify V14, V17, V10, and Amount as the most fraud-predictive features in the ULB dataset.6.6 Concept Drift and Real-Time ChallengesFewer than 15% of reviewed papers address concept drift—the change in statistical properties of fraud patterns over time. Pozzolo et al. (2020) provide the most rigorous treatment with realistic time-based splits and verification latency modelling. Real-time deployment requirements (sub-100ms inference) are rarely benchmarked, representing a significant gap between academic performance and production readiness.7.
